21. Can you explain the difference between Strong Consistency and Eventual Consistency?

What they are looking for: An understanding of the CAP theorem tradeoffs, how data synchronizes across multiple nodes, and when to use which approach.

Imagine you have a shared bank account with a partner, and you both check the balance at the same time from different ATMs. If the system uses Strong Consistency, the moment one of you withdraws money, the system effectively "locks" the data. The next read is guaranteed to see the updated balance. Both of you will always see the exact same, up-to-date number, but it might take a moment to load while the system double-checks everything across its servers.

With Eventual Consistency, it's like updating a decentralized ledger. You might withdraw money, and your partner might look at the balance a split second later and still see the old amount. However, the system guarantees that eventually (usually within milliseconds), all copies of the data will sync up and match. In our trading swarm, we use eventual consistency for fast-moving metrics (like real-time price aggregations) where speed is prioritized over absolute precision at every given microsecond.

22. What are CRDTs (Conflict-Free Replicated Data Types) and why are they useful?

What they are looking for: Familiarity with distributed data structures that resolve conflicts automatically without needing centralized locks.

Think of working on a shared Google Doc with a team. If everyone is typing at the same time offline and then suddenly reconnects, the system has to figure out how to merge all the changes without losing anyone's work or requiring a manual review of every single letter.

CRDTs are special data structures designed to do exactly this for distributed systems. They use specific mathematical rules to ensure that no matter what order updates arrive at different servers, all servers will eventually end up with the exact same final state. They are incredibly useful in distributed systems (like our trading swarm) because they eliminate the need for slow, centralized "locks" (where only one service can update a value at a time). This allows for high-availability, low-latency updates across Redis clusters.

23. How does a Last-Write-Wins (LWW) Register work?

What they are looking for: Understanding of a simple CRDT mechanism for resolving concurrent updates based on timestamps.

Imagine a guestbook at a hotel where guests write their favorite restaurant recommendation. If two guests try to write their recommendation on the exact same line at the exact same time, whose writing stays? A Last-Write-Wins (LWW) Register solves this by attaching a timestamp to every single update.

In a distributed system, if Node A updates a user's status to "Active" at 10:00:01 and Node B updates it to "Inactive" at 10:00:02, the LWW Register compares the timestamps. Because 10:00:02 is later, "Inactive" becomes the final state across the entire system. It’s a very simple and fast way to resolve conflicts, though it does require the servers to have relatively synchronized clocks (or use logical clocks) so that the "last" write is actually the most recent one.

24. Can you explain the mechanics of an Observed-Remove Set (ORSet)?

What they are looking for: Knowledge of how distributed systems handle adding and removing identical elements concurrently without accidental data loss.

Let's say you and your roommate share a digital grocery list. You both see "Apples" on the list. You decide you don't need them and delete "Apples". At the exact same moment, your roommate decides you need more apples and adds "Apples" to the list again. In a naive system, the deletion might overwrite the addition, and you get no apples.

An Observed-Remove Set (ORSet) solves this by giving a unique hidden ID (a tag) to every item when it is added. When your roommate added "Apples", the system saw it as "Apples-Tag1". When you removed it, you specifically removed "Apples-Tag1". If your roommate concurrently adds "Apples" again, it gets a new tag ("Apples-Tag2"). The system sees that you deleted Tag1, but Tag2 was just added, so "Apples" stays on the list. ORSets safely handle concurrent additions and deletions of the same data elements.

25. How do you prevent race conditions in a distributed system if you can't use CRDTs?

What they are looking for: Understanding of Distributed Locks and how to manage concurrency when absolute consistency is required.

A race condition happens when two parts of a system try to modify the same data at the same time, leading to unpredictable results. If you can't use CRDTs (for example, when decrementing a limited bank balance where you cannot allow it to go below zero), you need a Distributed Lock.

Imagine a single physical bathroom key in an office. If you have the key, you can use the room; everyone else must wait until you return it. In our architecture, we often use Redis to implement distributed locks (like the SETNX command - Set if Not eXists). A worker process asks Redis for the "key" to a specific task. If it gets it, it processes the task and then releases the key. If another worker tries to get the key while it's in use, it will be denied and must wait. This prevents two trading strategies from executing conflicting trades on the same portfolio simultaneously.

26. Explain the roles of Exchanges, Queues, and Routing Keys in RabbitMQ.

What they are looking for: Solid grasp of the AMQP (Advanced Message Queuing Protocol) messaging model and how messages flow from producers to consumers.

Think of RabbitMQ as a massive post office.

  • The Exchange is the sorting facility. When you send a letter (message), you don't send it directly to a house; you send it to the sorting facility.
  • The Routing Key is the zip code on the letter. It tells the sorting facility how to categorize the letter.
  • The Queue is the individual mailbox at someone's house.

When a producer (like our LangGraph AI Orchestrator) creates a task, it sends it to an Exchange with a Routing Key (e.g., "trade.execute"). The Exchange looks at its rules (bindings) and says, "Ah, messages with 'trade.execute' go into the Execution Engine Queue!" The consumers (our C++ workers) then pick up the messages from their respective queues.

27. In RabbitMQ, why is setting prefetch_count=1 often recommended for worker queues?

What they are looking for: Understanding of load balancing, fair dispatch, and avoiding worker starvation in message queues.

Imagine a teacher handing out 10 complex math problems to two students. If the teacher uses round-robin and hands 5 problems to Student A and 5 to Student B all at once (a high prefetch count), there is a risk. What if Student A's problems take 10 minutes each, but Student B's take 1 minute? Student B will finish in 5 minutes and sit idle, while Student A is overwhelmed for 50 minutes.

Setting prefetch_count=1 is like telling the teacher: "Only give a student one problem at a time. When they finish it and hand it in, give them the next one." This is called Fair Dispatch. In our C++ execution engine, some backtesting tasks are massive while others are tiny. With a prefetch of 1, fast workers continually pull new tasks from RabbitMQ as soon as they are free, ensuring maximum CPU utilization across the cluster.

28. How does RabbitMQ ensure Message Durability, and what is the performance tradeoff?

What they are looking for: Understanding of how to prevent data loss during system crashes and the disk I/O costs associated with it.

Imagine taking orders at a restaurant. If you memorize them (in-memory), it's incredibly fast, but if you suddenly faint (server crash), all the orders are lost. If you write them down in a notebook (disk storage), it takes a bit longer, but the orders survive even if you take a break.

In RabbitMQ, for absolute safety, you must declare both the Queue as Durable (the mailbox survives a restart) AND the Message as Persistent (the letter itself is written to disk). The tradeoff is performance. Writing to a physical disk (disk I/O) is significantly slower than keeping things in RAM. In a trading system, we might make critical trade execution tasks durable, but keep high-frequency, non-critical logging messages transient to save speed.

29. What is the difference between an ACK and a NACK in message queues, and what happens when a NACK occurs?

What they are looking for: Knowledge of consumer acknowledgements, fault tolerance, and Dead Letter Queues (DLQs).

When a delivery driver hands you a package, they usually ask you to sign for it. That signature is an ACK (Acknowledgement). It tells RabbitMQ, "I have successfully processed this message, you can safely delete it from the queue."

If the package is damaged or you refuse it, that's a NACK (Negative Acknowledgement). If a C++ worker encounters a fatal error while running a strategy, it sends a NACK to RabbitMQ. Based on configuration, RabbitMQ can either requeue the message (put it back in line to be tried again) or, more safely, route it to a Dead Letter Queue (DLQ). A DLQ is like an "undeliverable mail" bin where developers can later inspect the failed tasks to figure out why they crashed without blocking the rest of the system.

30. Why use Redis Pub/Sub for distributed state synchronization instead of RabbitMQ?

What they are looking for: The ability to choose the right tool for the job based on persistence and broadcasting requirements.

RabbitMQ is like sending a certified letter: it's guaranteed to be delivered, stored safely until read, and usually goes to one specific worker. It’s perfect for task queues.

Redis Pub/Sub is like a radio broadcast. The radio station (Publisher) broadcasts a song on a specific frequency (Channel). Anyone who has their radio turned on and tuned to that frequency (Subscribers) hears the song instantly. However, if your radio is turned off when the song plays, you miss it forever—Redis does not store the message for you.

We use Redis Pub/Sub for rapid distributed state synchronization (like broadcasting live market price ticks to multiple C++ nodes simultaneously) because it has incredibly low latency and high throughput. We don't need persistent storage for a price tick that will be obsolete in 100 milliseconds anyway; we just need it broadcasted as fast as possible.
